Impact.

Performance is how the site behaves. This is what it does to people: whether they stay, whether they play, and what they say. It is early, so this page is mostly how I will read the numbers, plus what people have already told me.

By Chuck Rice · 27 Oct 2026

01

What I count

I don't follow people. Two cookie-free tools count visits (Cloudflare) and what happens during them (Umami). The measure I care about most is engaged time: seconds the page is open and in front of you.

Umami can't average that for me, because a visit to one lab is a single page and reads as zero. So every page sends a small note as a visit passes 10 seconds, 30 seconds, 1 minute, 2 minutes and 5 minutes. I read the result as shares: what fraction of visitors to the dog got past a minute.

Rule: count moments, not people.

02

What the research says

Less than I hoped. There is good evidence about how quickly people decide to stay or go, and almost none about how long a toy should hold someone. Nielsen Norman Group looked at billions of page visits: the first 10 seconds decide whether you stay, the risk of leaving stays high until about 30 seconds, and after that it flattens. Chartbeat's 2014 analysis found 55% of visitors spent under 15 seconds actively on a page, though that was mostly news.

Rule: compare the labs with each other before comparing them with anyone else.

03

What I think, before the data

I'd rather write my guesses down now than explain them away later. Two, and what would prove each wrong.

1. Depth of time shows which lab people enjoyed. Nobody sits through two minutes of something they don't like. So the share of visitors who pass 60 seconds, and then 2 minutes, is my enjoyment signal, lab by lab. Going on the reactions so far, I expect the dog to come out ahead. I would like to be wrong, because that is the more interesting result.

Wrong if: a lab built to be watched, like the ocean, scores low only because people leave it open in another tab, or one that is quick to understand scores low because it was quick and satisfying.

2. Views are a rough measure of pull. The lab with the most views is the one people come back to and send to someone else. I'll count views per lab and treat them as a stand-in for repeat engagement.

Wrong if: the count is mostly me, friends and refreshes, or a lab is simply higher up the index and so gets seen first. Views mix first visits and returns, so I'll also look at visitors who came back, where the tool allows.

Together: depth says how much people liked it, views say whether they wanted it again. A lab that scores high on both is the keeper. One that scores on only one tells me something different, and I'd rather find that out than guess.

Rule: write the guess down first, and write down what would prove it wrong.

04

What people have told me

A handful of reactions from friends and colleagues, so a small and friendly sample. Several went straight to the birthday dog, and the neon sign on the landing page got a good response.

My daughter loves the dog. She blew out the candle again and again, and stroked the dog every time. No chart will show that. It is the same person doing the same thing on purpose, loads of times, which is the best sign I have.

Rule: a repeat is worth more than a minute.

05

What the numbers say

Nothing yet. Engagement tracking went live on 27 October. Once there are a few weeks of visits I will add, for each lab, the share of visitors who passed each mark, where they came from, and which write-ups get read to the end.

Rule: don't publish a number until there are enough visitors for it to mean something.

06

What I can't see

  • Enjoyment. A tab left open in the foreground counts as engaged, and someone who loved it for 20 seconds doesn't.
  • Return visits after a day. Without cookies, a returning visitor looks new.
  • Clean samples. My own visits, friends and bots all count, and early numbers will be small.
  • Background tabs. Time stops counting when the tab is hidden, on purpose.

Rule: say what the number can't tell you.
